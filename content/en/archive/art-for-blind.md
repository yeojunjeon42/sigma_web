## Overview

Visual impairment affects roughly 220 million people worldwide, many of whom lose their sight later in life. While people with visual impairments face barriers to appreciating visual art, their visual cortex remains intact even when ocular vision is limited.

Drawing on the concept of neuroplasticity, we developed an assistive art-viewing system that conveys visual artwork to the visual cortex through alternative senses such as touch and hearing. First, we used generalizable deep learning models to extract key artistic features—objects, figures, contours, and scene context—and built hardware that translates these features into multisensory feedback. Compared to traditional relief sculptures and audio guides, this approach substantially reduces production time and cost while grounding feature translation in aesthetic perception theory to align with how sighted viewers experience art. Combining multiple sensory modalities enables richer art appreciation for people with visual impairments.

## Motivation

As of 2018, approximately 250,000 people in Korea and 220 million people globally (2010 data) live with visual impairments. Cultural participation surveys show that visual art exhibitions have the lowest attendance among people with visual impairments due to sight-centric exhibition formats, highlighting the need for multisensory assistive devices. Existing aids—such as tactile word/color readers, relief reproductions, and audio descriptions—are expensive, time-consuming to produce, and rely on a single sensory channel.

## How it works

The device allows people with visual impairments to select artworks independently and explore them at their own pace. By using deep learning and structured mapping rules to convert image features automatically, the system avoids the high cost and labor of custom exhibition fabrication. The user experience consists of three stages: automated artwork feature conversion, artwork selection via the user interface, and multisensory exploration.

## Process

Our system delivers visual imagery through auditory and tactile cues, leveraging neuroplasticity—the brain's ability to reorganize neural pathways in response to sensory input. With training, people with visual impairments can process spatial and visual information through non-visual senses that activate the visual cortex, and multisensory neurons produce richer perception when multiple stimuli are presented together. Because artists and art movements vary widely in style, a single uniform translation rule can distort an artwork's character; therefore, our system tailors texture, scent, temperature, and vibration cues to specific artists and artistic styles.

## Impact

This device expands access to visual arts for people with visual impairments. With an estimated unit manufacturing cost of around 300,000 KRW, it provides a cost-effective alternative to single-exhibition tactile installations by allowing users to experience an unlimited number of artworks.
