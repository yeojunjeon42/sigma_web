## Overview

Currently, acoustic traffic signals are installed in Seoul to help people with visual impairments cross crosswalks. However, these signals suffer from safety risks, malfunctions, and other issues, leading to long-standing calls for improvement. Our team developed a new signaling aid device and mobile application to replace acoustic traffic signals that are difficult for people with visual impairments to use when crossing the street.

We improved tactile paving blocks, which currently provide directional information to people with visual impairments while walking. Linear tactile blocks indicate "proceed," while dome-shaped tactile blocks indicate "stop." Normally, linear blocks run along the sidewalk and transition to dome-shaped blocks at crosswalks. We developed a tactile block that can take the form of both dome-shaped and linear blocks: when the pedestrian signal is red, it takes the dome-shaped form to signal "stop," and when the signal turns green, it transforms into the linear form to signal "proceed."

## Motivation

To develop a device that replaces acoustic traffic signals, we focused on two requirements: first, the device must be easily accessible to people with visual impairments, and second, users must be able to intuitively detect signal changes. To meet both criteria, we decided to convey pedestrian signal information using tactile paving blocks that people with visual impairments already rely on while walking.

We adopted the way existing tactile blocks communicate information. We designed a new tactile block, named "Didim Block," that transforms from dome-shaped to linear and back so it can perform both roles. Replacing the dome-shaped tactile blocks currently installed in front of crosswalks with this device will effectively deliver pedestrian signal information to people with visual impairments.

## How it works

Tactile paving blocks convey information to people with visual impairments in two ways. Linear blocks indicate that it is safe to proceed in the direction of the lines, while dome-shaped blocks warn of the end of the sidewalk or an upcoming crosswalk and signal the user to stop. Because both types are installed nationwide, we determined that people with visual impairments have no difficulty distinguishing between them. Therefore, if the dome-shaped blocks in front of a crosswalk convert into linear blocks when the pedestrian signal turns green, users will feel the change from domes to lines and know the signal has changed. Replacing existing dome-shaped blocks at crosswalks with these mechanical tactile blocks eliminates the need to install separate guiding blocks leading to acoustic signal poles, and users do not have to search for and press a button to check the signal.

## Process

First, because pedestrians step on sidewalk blocks, the overall structure had to withstand heavy loads. In addition, since a complex drive mechanism would make the block too thick, we designed a simple mechanism with high torque. We designed the spaces between the domes to move vertically up and down, converting the surface between dome-shaped and linear forms, and implemented this motion using a linear stepper motor.

## Impact

Traveling safely to a destination without assistance is vital for people with visual impairments. Crossing a crosswalk carries much higher risk than walking on a regular sidewalk because of moving vehicles. Our application and Didim Block make crossing crosswalks safer and more convenient for people with visual impairments. Furthermore, because the beacon-based location guidance service can be applied not only at crosswalks but also on regular sidewalks, it can be expanded into a broader pedestrian navigation interface for people with visual impairments.
